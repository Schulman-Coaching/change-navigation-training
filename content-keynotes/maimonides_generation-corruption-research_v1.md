# Maimonides on "Constantly Coming Into Existence and Ceasing to Exist"

Research brief tracing the source phrase across the Rambam's corpus.

**Assumption stated up front:** "the attached" = *Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah* 2:3 and 4:5, the two files in this folder. The phrase you flagged — "constantly coming into existence and ceasing to exist" — is Touger's rendering of הֹוִים וְנִפְסָדִים תָּמִיד (*hovim ve-nifsadim tamid*) in 2:3.

---

## What the phrase actually names

It is not a poetic flourish. It is a technical term: the Aristotelian doctrine of **generation and corruption** — *havayah ve-hefsed* in Hebrew, *kawn wa-fasād* in the Judeo-Arabic Maimonides wrote in, after Aristotle's *On Generation and Corruption*. Everything below the sphere of the moon that is a **composite of matter and form** is, by definition, subject to it. To be made of matter is to be on loan from it.

In 2:3 this is the *first* of three tiers of creation:

- **Matter + form, in flux** → "constantly coming into existence and ceasing to exist" — bodies, beasts, plants, metals.
- **Matter + form, fixed** → the spheres and stars, which do not pass away.
- **Form without matter** → the angels / separate intellects.

The dividing line through all three is the same single variable: *how much matter, and how loosely it holds its form.* That is the spine of the whole passage.

---

## Where the idea recurs in the Rambam (in order of usefulness for teaching)

### 1. Mishneh Torah, Yesodei HaTorah 3–4 (the immediate continuation)
2:3 states the taxonomy; chapters 3 and 4 prove it.

- **3:1–9** — the nine spheres and the stars: matter and form, but *permanently fixed*, so they never undergo generation and corruption. The contrast case that makes 2:3's first tier meaningful.
- **4:1–5** (your second file) — the four elements (earth, water, wind, fire) "in a constant state of flux… changing into another every day and every hour… an unceasing recurrent cycle" (*ḥozrot ḥalilah le-olam*). This is the *mechanism* underneath "coming into existence and ceasing to exist": every composite body is built from elements that are themselves never at rest.
- **4:6** draws the explicit conclusion: everything composed of the four elements is "born and perishes" (*hoveh ve-nifsad*).

Teaching note: 2:3 and 4:5 are the same doctrine stated at two zoom levels — 2:3 names the category, 4:5 shows the churn that produces it.

### 2. Guide of the Perplexed III:8 — matter as the "married harlot"
The strongest and most quotable development. Matter is "never found without form… like a married woman who is never without a husband, and yet, though wedded, constantly seeks another man in place of her husband." Matter can never hold one form; it is always casting off what it has to put on the next. Therefore "**all corruption, destruction, or defect comes from matter.**" Mortality is not added to material things from outside — it *is* materiality, seen over time.

### 3. Guide III:10 — corruption is privation, and privation was never "created"
Evil, death, and decay are not positive things God made; they are *absences* — the flip side of a thing's coming-to-be. This reframes "ceasing to exist" from a defect in creation into the necessary shadow of generation.

### 4. Guide III:12 — the un-see-it move
The argument that flips the phrase on its head: "**genesis can only take place through destruction, and without the destruction of the individual members of the species the species themselves would not exist permanently.**" If a thing never changed and never died, there would be one frozen being and no *species* — no individuals at all. Death is the price of plurality. The sublunar world's "ceasing to exist" is the very thing that lets a "coming into existence" keep happening. Decay is not the failure of the system; it is the system.

### 5. Eight Chapters (Shemoneh Perakim) 1 & 4 — the same physics applied to the human
Maimonides maps matter/form onto body/soul: a person's defects "are all due to the substance of the body and not to its form," while knowledge, virtue, and the image of God belong to the form. The 2:3 cosmology becomes an ethics — the part of you that is "constantly ceasing to exist" is the part to govern, not to obey.

### 6. Guide I:72 — the cosmos as one organism
The whole universe is a single body; the sublunar realm of generation and corruption sits inside it the way perishable tissue sits inside a living animal whose overall form endures. Useful if you want the macro frame around 2:3's micro categories.

### 7. Guide II:27–29 — the world itself does *not* perish
Important boundary: individuals in the sublunar world come to be and pass away, but the *world as a whole* is not destined for destruction. Keeps "ceasing to exist" from being mistaken for cosmic pessimism. The churn is permanent; the churning thing endures.

---

## The throughline (the keynote-usable idea)

Read flat, "constantly coming into existence and ceasing to exist" sounds like a verdict on how fragile the lowest things are. Read the way Maimonides builds it out, the verdict inverts: *that constant ceasing is what makes a world of individuals possible at all.* The spheres are deathless and therefore barren of new individuals; the angels have no matter and so nothing ever happens to them. Only the bottom tier — the one defined by loss — is the tier where birth, change, growth, and generation occur. The price of being able to become is being unable to remain.

That is the line that, once seen, you can't un-see in 2:3: Maimonides has filed *us* under the category whose defining feature is impermanence, and then spends three more chapters showing that this is not the booby prize. It is the only address in the universe where new things are born.
